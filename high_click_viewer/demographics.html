2. High-Click Participants: Demographic Profile

The 33 high-click participants (≥ 95th percentile mean actions, ≥ 4 solved puzzles) are compared against the full sample on gender, education, age, and race. Because the high-click group is small (n = 33), these comparisons are descriptive — differences may not reach statistical significance, but they point to potentially interesting patterns.

  • Gender skew: Women make up 54.8% of high-click participants compared to 43.8% overall, suggesting women are modestly overrepresented among exploratory clickers. Non-binary participants are also overrepresented (6.5% vs 1.0%).
  • No age effect: HC mean age (40.3 years) is virtually identical to the overall mean (39.6 years), spanning age groups 25–34 through 55–64 evenly.
  • Doctorate overrepresentation: 6.9% of HC participants hold a doctorate (vs 2.7% overall — 2.5× the base rate), while graduate-degree holders are underrepresented (6.9% vs 15.7%). The majority of HC participants (48.3%) hold undergraduate degrees, mirroring the sample composition.
  • Race broadly proportional: 72.4% of HC participants identify as White (vs 71.7% overall). Asian participants are slightly overrepresented (10.3% vs 6.9%), while Black/African American participants are underrepresented (3.4% vs 10.1%), though the small HC sample size limits interpretation.

3. Off-Path Steps by Demographic Group

For each of the 1,275 participants with at least one solved, complete attempt, off-path behaviour was characterised as the mean number of off-path edit steps per solved task. The charts below show how this metric varies across gender, age group, and education level. Higher values indicate more exploratory or error-prone clicking per puzzle.

  • Gender — modest differences: Non-binary participants average 3.22 off-path steps per task, compared to 2.40 for women and 2.16 for men. Women make slightly more off-path errors than men across the board. The non-binary finding is based on n = 14 and should be treated cautiously.
  • Age — U-shaped pattern: The youngest group (18–24) makes the most off-path steps per task (3.32), efficiency improves steadily through to 45–54 (1.89 — the most accurate group), then rises again at 55–64 (2.59) and 65+ (2.98). This suggests a performance peak in middle age, with more exploratory behavior at both ends of the age range.
  • Education — non-linear relationship: Off-path frequency does not decrease monotonically with education. Doctoral participants have the highest mean (3.44), followed by technical college (2.75), while high school diploma holders are the most on-path (2.11). This suggests higher education is associated with more exploratory problem-solving, not simply greater accuracy.
  • Overall interpretation: Demographic groups that tend toward more off-path steps (young adults, doctoral-level participants, non-binary respondents) may explore the solution space more actively — consistent with the earlier finding that off-path behaviour reflects structured exploration rather than random error.
