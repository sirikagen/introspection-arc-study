  • Gender skew: Women make up 54.8% of high-click participants compared to 43.8% overall, suggesting women are modestly overrepresented among exploratory clickers. Non-binary participants are also overrepresented (6.5% vs 1.0%).
  • No age effect: HC mean age (40.3 years) is virtually identical to the overall mean (39.6 years), spanning age groups 25–34 through 55–64 evenly.
  • Doctorate overrepresentation: 6.9% of HC participants hold a doctorate (vs 2.7% overall — 2.5× the base rate), while graduate-degree holders are underrepresented (6.9% vs 15.7%). The majority of HC participants (48.3%) hold undergraduate degrees, mirroring the sample composition.
  • Race broadly proportional: 72.4% of HC participants identify as White (vs 71.7% overall). Asian participants are slightly overrepresented (10.3% vs 6.9%), while Black/African American participants are underrepresented (3.4% vs 10.1%), though the small HC sample size limits interpretation.

  • Gender — modest differences: Non-binary participants average 3.22 off-path steps per task, compared to 2.40 for women and 2.16 for men. Women make slightly more off-path errors than men across the board. The non-binary finding is based on n = 14 and should be treated cautiously.
  • Age — U-shaped pattern: The youngest group (18–24) makes the most off-path steps per task (3.32), efficiency improves steadily through to 45–54 (1.89 — the most accurate group), then rises again at 55–64 (2.59) and 65+ (2.98). This suggests a performance peak in middle age, with more exploratory behavior at both ends of the age range.
  • Education — non-linear relationship: Off-path frequency does not decrease monotonically with education. Doctoral participants have the highest mean (3.44), followed by technical college (2.75), while high school diploma holders are the most on-path (2.11). This suggests higher education is associated with more exploratory problem-solving, not simply greater accuracy.
  • Overall interpretation: Demographic groups that tend toward more off-path steps (young adults, doctoral-level participants, non-binary respondents) may explore the solution space more actively — consistent with the earlier finding that off-path behaviour reflects structured exploration rather than random error.

4. The "Modal" Profile: White, Man, Undergraduate, Age 35–44

The combination of White race, male gender, undergraduate education, and age 35–44 represents the single most common demographic intersection in this sample. This section asks whether that profile is over- or under-represented among high-click participants and among participants who made off-path steps, compared to the overall sample.

Shown as percentage of each group's total. The near-identical rates (~6%) across all three groups indicate that the modal demographic profile carries no special predictive weight for high-click or off-path behaviour.

  • No overrepresentation in HC: 6.1% of high-click participants match all four criteria, compared to 6.4% of all participants — a negligible difference. Being White, male, undergraduate-educated, and aged 35–44 does not increase the likelihood of being a high-click participant.
  • Marginal elevation in off-path group: 6.9% of off-path participants match all four criteria vs 6.4% overall — a difference of 0.5 percentage points. This is not meaningfully different, confirming that this demographic profile does not predict off-path behaviour.
  • Criterion-level patterns are consistent: Within the 35–44 age band, the proportion of White participants (~60%), men (~53–62%), and undergraduates (~40–54%) is broadly similar across all three groups, with the HC group showing a slightly higher undergraduate rate (53.8% vs 39.6% overall).
  • Overall conclusion: The modal demographic profile — the most statistically "typical" participant — is neither more nor less likely to exhibit high-click or high off-path behaviour. Individual variation within demographic groups accounts for far more variance than demographic group membership.

5. Profiles Prevalent Across All Three Groups

Rather than looking at one characteristic at a time, this section asks: which combinations of 3 or more demographic characteristics appear at a similar or elevated rate across all three groups simultaneously — total sample, high-click participants, and off-path participants? Every possible 3-way and 4-way combination of gender, race, education, and age group was evaluated. A profile is counted as "consistently prevalent" when both the HC rate and the off-path rate are at or above 90% of the overall sample rate.

  • Man + White + Age 35–44 is the single most stable high-prevalence profile across all three groups: 16.4% of all participants, 17.2% of HC, and 16.9% of off-path participants — a spread of just 0.9 percentage points. Being a White man in the core middle-age band appears in each group at almost identical rates.
  • Woman + White + 35–44 is even more stable in absolute terms (0.4 pp spread: 10.6% / 10.3% / 10.2%), confirming that middle-aged White participants of both genders are a persistent, evenly distributed backbone of the sample across all behavioural groups.
  • Woman + White + Undergraduate is the biggest outlier: it accounts for 13.9% of all participants and 13.7% of off-path participants, but 27.6% of high-click participants — nearly double the base rate. White women with an undergraduate degree are markedly overrepresented among the most exploratory clickers.
  • White + Undergraduate + Age 25–34 and White + Undergraduate + Age 35–44 are both consistently prevalent (≤ 5 pp spread), suggesting that White participants with undergraduate degrees — regardless of age within the 25–44 range — form a stable, behaviourally non-distinctive core across all groups.
  • No 4-way profile is equally distributed: every 4-way combination with an HC match shows a higher HC rate than the overall rate, which is partly a small-sample artefact (any single HC participant contributes ~3.4% to the HC figure). The most naturally elevated 4-way profile is Woman + White + Undergraduate + 35–44 (4.5% overall, 10.3% HC, 4.7% off-path), driven by the underlying Woman + White + Undergraduate overrepresentation in HC.
