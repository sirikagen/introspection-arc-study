Introspection — ARC-AGI

Clicking Behaviour and Semantics

Participants wrote a short description before submitting each puzzle attempt. This page analyses how the content and structure of those descriptions relate to the clicking patterns observed in the same attempt — and what changes between a first and final description reveal about how understanding evolves.

Dataset and Approach

Each puzzle attempt in the viewer includes a first written description (composed before the first submission) and a last written description (optionally updated after feedback). Semantic features were extracted from these free-text fields using keyword matching across five categories: rule-oriented, colour-oriented, spatial, quantitative, and epistemic uncertainty. These were then correlated with the clicking behaviour recorded in the same attempt.

All 217 records represent solved, complete attempts from participants in the high-click viewer. Because the sample is drawn primarily from high-click participants (n = 156 HC, 61 non-HC), findings are best understood as describing this specific population rather than all participants. Solve rate is 100% across all groups since only completed attempts are included.

1. Description Length and Clicking Volume

Does the length of a participant's description predict how many clicks they made? First-description word count was binned into four groups and compared against the mean total action count and mean undo count for each group.

Mean total actions — by first-description length

Non-linear relationship: medium-length descriptions (10–19 words) coincide with the highest action count.

Mean undo count — by first-description length

Very short descriptions have the highest undo rate; longer descriptions trend towards fewer corrections.

  • The relationship between description length and action count is non-linear: participants who write 10–19 words make the most clicks on average (98.8), while short (1–9 words: 82.6) and longer descriptions (20–39 words: 85.6) both coincide with fewer clicks.
  • Very brief descriptions (1–9 words) have the highest undo rate (2.9 mean undos), suggesting that participants who write less may be more uncertain in their clicking — editing and correcting more often.
  • Longer descriptions (40+ words) are associated with moderate action counts (93.8) and low undos (1.2), pointing to participants who plan carefully and execute with relatively few corrections.

2. Revising the Description: A Strong Behavioural Marker

Participants could optionally update their written description after seeing the outcome. 64 of 216 attempts (30%) resulted in a changed description. This revision behaviour shows a striking relationship with the clicking pattern in the same attempt.

Among the 64 revised attempts, 69% wrote more words in the final description (mean 19.0 → 25.0 words). 28% condensed their description (fewer words in the last).

  • Participants who revised their description used half the total clicks compared to those who kept it the same (53.3 vs 106.5 actions) — the single strongest semantic–behavioural correlation in the dataset.
  • The revised group also has more than twice the undo rate (3.4 vs 1.3). Combining both signals: revision-associated attempts involve more active course-correction (undos) but substantially fewer total clicks — suggesting that participants who ultimately revise their understanding are more reflexive and more efficient, even within the same attempt.
  • When participants do revise, 69% write more in the last description (mean word count grows from 19 to 25). A minority (28%) condense it — typically simplifying a first attempt's verbose speculation into a cleaner rule statement.
  • The unchanged-description group is dominated by high-click participants (HC), who tend to maintain consistent high-level descriptions across many exploratory clicks without needing to update their stated understanding. This suggests high-click behaviour is not accompanied by conceptual confusion — the description stays the same because the participant's interpretation of the puzzle remains stable.

3. Semantic Category and Clicking Behaviour

Five semantic categories were identified by keyword matching in the first written description: rule/transformation language, colour language, spatial language, quantitative/count language, and epistemic uncertainty language. For each category, clicking behaviour was compared between attempts that do and do not contain those keywords.

  • Spatial language → dramatically fewer undos: Attempts where participants describe positions (row, column, edge, diagonal, etc.) produce a mean of just 0.6 undos, compared to 2.8 for attempts without spatial language — a 4.7× difference. Spatial reasoning appears to guide more precise clicking.
  • Rule-oriented language → more total clicks: Descriptions that name transformation rules (mirror, repeat, rotate, extend…) are associated with 114 mean actions vs 80 without. These attempts involve complex spatial transformations that genuinely require more clicking, not just more exploration.
  • Colour language → fewer clicks than no-colour: Participants who explicitly name colours in their description (84.6 mean actions) click less than those who don't (100.0), suggesting that colour-oriented framings are associated with relatively tractable, visually guided tasks.
  • Uncertainty language → more clicks, fewer undos: Participants who express uncertainty (maybe, think, unsure, tried, wrong…) make more total actions (104.2 vs 89.0) but — counter-intuitively — fewer undos (0.9 vs 2.0). They persist without course-correcting, consistent with a trial-and-error rather than plan-and-execute strategy.

TTR measures lexical diversity in the description — a high TTR (≥ 0.8) means most words are unique; a low TTR (< 0.8) indicates more repetition. Across the full dataset:

High-TTR descriptions (n = 134) are associated with more actions (95.1) and more undos (2.5). Low-TTR descriptions (n = 82) come with fewer clicks (83.7) and fewer undos (1.1), suggesting that more repetitive descriptions correspond to simpler, more routine tasks.

  • HC participants write descriptions that are 26% longer on average (23.0 vs 18.2 words), and this extends to final descriptions as well (24.9 vs 19.7 words). They invest more in articulating their understanding.
  • HC participants use colour keywords more often (65% vs 54%), consistent with the finding that off-path errors in HC participants are primarily colour-selection errors — colour features are salient to them both in action and in description.
  • HC participants are more likely to revise their description (32% vs 23%), suggesting greater introspective engagement across the attempt — despite (or because of) their higher click volume.
  • HC and non-HC participants use rule-oriented keywords at nearly identical rates (30% vs 33%), suggesting that abstract transformation language is not what distinguishes high-click behaviour — the difference lies in richness and precision of description, not the type of reasoning expressed.

5. How Descriptions Change Across Retry Attempts

When a participant retries the same puzzle, does their description become more or less structured? Descriptions from first, second, and third attempts were compared on length and semantic keyword rates.

0% Attempt 3 attempts with spatial keywords (vs 44% on attempt 1)

Mean description word count by attempt number

Descriptions shorten monotonically with each retry.

Keyword category rates (%) by attempt number

Rule, spatial, and count keywords all decline on retries. Uncertainty language peaks at attempt 2 (12%) before dropping at attempt 3.

  • Descriptions shorten with every retry: from 22.9 words on the first attempt to 19.5 on the second and 16.4 on the third — a 28% reduction from attempt 1 to attempt 3. Participants write less as they grow familiar with (or frustrated by) a puzzle.
  • Spatial keywords collapse completely by attempt 3: 44% of first attempts contain spatial language, 31% of second attempts, and 0% of third attempts. This is the sharpest semantic shift across retries and suggests that explicit spatial reasoning in descriptions is abandoned when the puzzle is not solved after two tries.
  • Rule-oriented language also declines (34% → 24% → 21%), indicating that participants on retries describe less in terms of abstract transformation rules and more in concrete or minimal terms — possibly a signal of cognitive offloading to the clicking process itself.
  • Uncertainty language peaks at attempt 2 (12%, up from 9% at attempt 1), then falls at attempt 3 (7%). This non-monotonic pattern suggests that participants acknowledge uncertainty most explicitly after one failed attempt, but become more resigned — or more pragmatic — by a third try.
